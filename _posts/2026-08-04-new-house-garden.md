Posting a quick status update, as I haven't had a chance to continue working on my non-profit concept in a few weeks. This is because I'm in the process of buying a house and I've been primarily focused 
on preparations for that. I hope to return to the non-profit concept in the next few months, but in the meantime, the new house has given me a chance to learn a lot about designing a native plant garden/landscape. 

I've wanted to make my own native plant garden for some time now, but renting has made that impossible. Now though, given that the house has a spacious backyard and no HOA, I'm  excited to begin deploying what I've
learned about native-plant ecology. While I haven't yet physically inspected the conditions in the backyard, I've developed a general outline that I can refine as I learn more, a "plant palette" so-to-speak. The plants listed below were derived from various sources, but I started with a list of native keystone species I found and then enhanced that through further research. My goal is to balance ecological contribution and human appeal.

First off, the property currently has four trees: three Bradford Pears and a Cabbage Palm. The Bradford Pear is an invasive species that's become quite common throughout North America. It grows quickly, is hardy in most climates, and is considered  aesthetically pleasing. However, my understanding is that it offers limited ecological value. Birds eat the fruits (spreading the seeds long distances) and pollinators can somewhat use the nectar, but that seems to be where the positives end. Its dense growth can inhibit native plants while not offering the range of services native trees offer, in particular the hosting of insect larvae. Plus, while I agree they are quite pretty when blooming, the flowers simultaneously smell unpleasant. All to say, I intend to cut down these trees in favor of new native tree species. The cabbage palm is native and offers many services to local wildlife, so I'll leave that where it is. 

To replace the Bradford pears, I plan to plant two Black Gum (AKA Black Tupelo) seedlings in the late fall. Black Gums are extremely valuable ecologically. The berries they produce are nutritious to wildlife, the nectar is used by pollinators, several moth species use it as a larva host, and mature specimens often hollow out, providing shelter to squirrels and other small mammals. Aside from that it provides great shade and is gorgeous in the fall when the leaves change. I also understand that they are protected by city ordinance which should make it harder for anyone to remove them later on. 

I'd like to also install a Service-berry or Eastern Red-bud to replace the third Bradford, but I'm still thinking on where and which one. The Red-bud is also protected by city ordinances but I understand can be a little high-maintenance compared to the Serviceberry. Likely the soil conditions will end up making that choice for me. 

Aside from the trees, I have a list of small trees and shrubs I want to plant where I can. I know I definitely want to plant Black-haw Viburnum and Sparkle-berry shrubs in the backyard (2 of each to increase odds of cross-pollination and fruit production). Other woody plants I'm interested in are American Hazelnut, Beautyberry, Dwarf Fothergilla, Virginia Sweetspire, and Spicebush. These are all native shrubs or small trees of high ecological value. 

While doing my research into native garden design, I quickly found out that a garden filled only with flowering herbaceous perennials, while certainly better than a turf-grass mono-culture lawn, isn't really the ideal end-goal in terms of ecological benefit. Instead what you want is variety, and variety in every sense: plant height, leaf type, bloom color, bloom season, etc. Indeed, while the turf grasses we tend to see throughout suburban landscapes offer minimal ecological benefit, the same is not true for native grasses and sedges, and in fact they are vital aspects to a balanced ecosystem. Some sedges or grasses I'm considering from my garden are: 

- Little Bluestem
- Wooly Bulrush
- Eastern Wild-Rye
- Switchgrass
- White-Topped Sedge
- Pink Muhly Grass
- Deer-Tongue Witchgrass
- River Oats

Which ones I select will depend on sun and soil conditions, but I know I want a shorter grass to plant with my shorter herbaceous perennials and then a taller grass to plant with taller perennials. A few of the herbaceous perennials on my list can also serve as ground-covers, so what I may do is use one or two of those as essentially a third "Grass", using them as the matrix specimen for the lowest layer in the garden. 

Next are my native vines, which I would plan to try and grow around certain sections of the house and on our backyard fence. These include:

- Carolina Jessamine
- Passionflower
- Crossvine
- Coral Honeysuckle

In terms of placement what I'll want to consider is "twining" vs "adhesive" growth. My understanding is of these, the Crossvine grows via adhesive tendrils and therefore would not be a good idea to plant it so that is grows on the house, as it's inclined to get under the vinyl siding. Instead the Crossvine will probably go on the fence in the backyard. I'll likely put Passionflower on the fence as well, for the same reasons. I would love to be able to train Carolina Jessamine and Coral Honeysuckle plants to grow around and over our front patio. Those vines grow via twining, grabbing onto whatever is within reach and is therefore less likely to damage the siding. 

Finally my list of herbaceous perennials: 

- Green-And-Gold (potential groundcover)
- Various Native Aster species
- Black-eyed Susans
- Purple Coneflower
- Spotted Beebalm
- Native Blazing Star Species
- Native Goldenrod Species
- Native Joe Pye Weed Species
- Eastern Columbine
- Native Salvias
- Native Hibiscus Species
- Native Milkweed/Asclepius species
- Native Violet Species (potential groundcovers)
- Native Sunflower Species
- Native Mountain-Mint Species

I don't expect I'll be able to plant all or even most of the species on my list, but I suspect that identifying the site conditions will narrow this list down substantially anyway.
