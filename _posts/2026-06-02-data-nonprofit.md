I don't think I've posted about this, but I recently earned my Master's Degree in Information Systems Technology. I feel extremely grateful to my family and friends for supporting
me throughout my studies, along with my wonderful employer for handling my tuition. I never want to lose sight of the good fortune and privilege I've enjoyed that allowed me to achieve 
this, and I feel a strong desire to make good use of the knowledge I've gained, both in my job and in my community. One idea I've been kicking around for a while now centers around establishing some sort of organization that would interface with local governmental bodies
in order to make the case for robust open data systems and help them establish these systems. As I'll detail below, this core idea has evolved a bit into, potentially,
a non-profit group that advocates for strong transparency, accessibility, and accountability policies at the local government level while offering technical guidance in furtherance of these principles. 

In the final course for my master's degree, I performed an analysis on a sampling of municipal open data systems, specifically through the dimensions of architecture, governance, and security.
In selecting which portals to analyze, I made sure to select portals operated by cities of varying sizes, from as large as New York City to as small as Scottsdale, AZ. 
Using several relevant research papers, I compiled a set of yes/no questions, in the categories of architecture, governance, and security, that I then used to evaluated each portal.
Ultimately I found that while there is much commonality in the basic features and architecture of these portals, governance and security policies lack standardization, requiring cities to essentially start from scratch in those areas. 

As part of my analysis, I also looked at the various reasons a municipality might want to etasblish an open data system in the first place. 
I found that aside from the obvious furtherance of government transparency (in my view, a worthy goal by itself), there are also many economic benefits. 
Specifically I looked at how the publication of city-issued building permits might enable real estate developers, contractors, and construction companies to 
enhance their market research capabilities in many regards. This ultimately could lead to these entities more effectively allocated resources and a resulting economic boost to the local land development industry. 

All to say, while I worked on this project, I began to think about my own local governments and how I could apply what I was learning to them. 
The City of Conway currently lacks an open data portal, although the GIS department does publish and maintain a small set of datasets. 
Horry County has an [ArcGIS-based portal](https://hcdatahub-hcg.hub.arcgis.com/search), but this offers a fairly minimal number of datasets, and they don't seem to be maintained very well. 

Another area that could use improvement is in making government activity and decisions transparent and understandable. 
This stems from my observations in [this post from last month](https://schi-427.github.io/ScarletSage.github.io/blog/urban-planning-1/), where I observed how there was no clear mechanisms dedicated to 
keeping the public informed on the status of the recently-adopted Parks and Recreation Master Plan. Although a member of the public *could* investigate council meeting minutes
to determine which actions have been taken in relation to this plan, I feel that the effort required to do so is frankly unreasonable. These are major, exciting city improvements and projects
and the public should be able to quickly understand what progress has been made towards them, or whether major roadblocks have come up. 


So basically this comes down to doing more than just posting council meeting documents (as Conway currently does), but going further to actively helping the public 
understand what their government is doing, including what actions are being considered, who voted for what, and what the status of ongoing projects is. 
Some ideas I've had for this include:

- A council agendas, packets, and minutes page that allows for searching
- A system for tracking council votes
- Plain-language ordinance summaries
- Intuitively-arranged dashboards for major projects and initiatives

Another observation I've made is centered on how local government seem to highly rely on private social media services, perhaps to the detriment of service execution and public engagement. 
Specifically what I have in mind is how the [Keep Conway Beautiful](https://www.conwaysc.gov/departments/beautification/index.php) commission, responsible for the " beautification and maintenance activities for all City properties,"
uses its [Facebook page](https://www.facebook.com/KeepConwayBeautiful/) to handle all volunteer activities. Likewise, the city's Parks and Recreation department seems 
to communicate with the public solely through [its own Facebook page](https://www.facebook.com/ConwayPRT). While this is understandable (Facebook pages are easy to set up, free of charge, user friendly, and the platform has a massive user-base)
ultimately I feel this strategy prevents citizens who do not use Facebook from participating in valuable civic events and receiving important information. While I'm sure
this wasn't the intended message, implicitly this tells the public that the city is not interested in engaging those who aren't on Facebook. 

My thoughts on solving this issue don't involve city departments leaving private social media services, but rather ensuring that citizens who aren't on these platforms have the same opportunity to 
receive important information and to engage with civic activities. I would envision a centralized city-owned webpage that hosts all news, alerts, and announcements from the city, perhaps filterable by department. 
Citizens should also be able to sign up for email alerts, again from the city as a whole or from each department. In this way, the municipality could continue leveraging valuable private
social media services without overly relying on them. 

Lastly, I feel there's a strong case to be made for including data related to oversight and ethics in a municipal open data system. If our overall goal is to
make governmental activity more readily understandable to the public, then the same should go for issues that might impact that activity. For instance,
if a council member receives a campaign donation from someone who later asks for and receives a zoning decision, or perhaps a council member votes to approve a new housing development, where the builder happens to be related to them, then this should be readily apparent to the public
without them having to dig into the state-mandated campaign finance reports. 
My idea for this would involve essentially "localizing" the state's ethics system such that
all documents related to local officials are copied and published locally. I admit I'm much less knowledgeable about this area compared to the previous topics, so more research is needed. 

Ultimately this lands me at four broad "pillars" for my non-profit:

1. Open Data: government data is published for public use by default.
2. Transparent Governance: government decisions should be visible and understandable.
3. Accessible Communication: public information should be easy to find and consume.
4. Public Accountability: citizens should be able to evaluate the performance and conduct of public officials.

Considering many non-profits have a set of "guiding principles" that shape their actions, these principles could be:

1. A healthy democracy relies on an informed citizenry
2. Public information belongs to the public
3. Transparency enables accountability
4. Accessibility and availability must be valued equally
5. Civic participation enhances government
6. Trust is earned through openness
7. Technology should strengthen civic institutions
