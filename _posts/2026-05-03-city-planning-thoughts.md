Lately I've been getting more interested in urban planning as a concept, probably stemming from my work in open data. If you're not aware, open data just means data that is made freely availible to anyone, with 
no restrictions on how it can be used. Commonly, this data is made availible in government-owned open data portals. So for instance if for some reason you wanted data about building permit issuance in the 
city of Scottsdale, Arizona, you can go [to their open data portal and grab it very easily](https://data.scottsdaleaz.gov/datasets/68ba7b38073f4cd4aee38d2b59afcaf4_12/explore). A major part of my 
recent captstone project was discussing how this data can provide insight to real estate developers, construction companies, contractors, and regular city residents. Other datasets can include 
things like police reports, business licenses, or traffic data. 

The implications of this kind of data on urban planning is pretty self-evident. Taken as a whole, the data provided by open data portals can provide incredible insight on how any given city is being run. 
As I touched on in my greenspace project, residential access to greenspace becomes much more easily determined through the parks, parcels, and zoning datasets, for instance. Many cities have datasets
dedicated to the trees that exist on public property, which could enable analysis centered on bio-diversity. Heat island analaysis could be performed using temperature measurements from local sensors, 
in combination with satellite imagery. Success trends of city events could be determined by analyzing attendance figures, perhaps even running analysis to see if events on certain days or certain seasons are more successful.

My greenspaces research, and the findings that greenspace access was indeed a major issue in my city, led me to the happy realizaiton that the local government was already addressing this issue with the
[Conway Parks and Recreation Master Plan](https://cms1files.revize.com/conway/2025%20Conway%20Parks%20and%20Recreation%20Master%20Plan.pdf?t=20260130122618926&t=20260130122618926). Although I haven't finished reading it, 
this strikes me as a very thorough plan and great step in the right direction. This document led me to the earlier-adopted [Conway Pathways and Trails Plan](https://cms1files.revize.com/conway/Conway%20Pathways%20and%20Trails%20Plan.pdf). 
This document also seems like a great development, although I was dissappointed to see that my neighborhood doesn't seem in line to receive any new trails or pathways. 

It's a great feeling to realize that your local government is already addressing many of the concerns that you have. That said, I do see some room for improvement, both with the parks plan itself, and the way it's being implement.
These are:

- Native plants are only mentioned once, and that only in a community garden context.
- No reference to regaular updates for stakeholders.

I view public spaces as a fantastic opportunity to promote and engage in the cultivation and protection of native wildlife and plants. This not only supports the native ecosystem, but can provide examples of
actionable conservation to local residents, who then may be inspired to cultivate native plants on the residential level. 

I also feel that ideally, a plan like this would have associated, dedicated update mechanisms, basically ways for interested parties to easily understand what progress has been made towards the goals of the plan. 
I would envision a dedicated webpage managed by the city that shares related updates, as well as a more thorough progress report released annually by the city, which details things like budget expense, any changes 
in the plan, setbacks and delays, etc. Perhaps the webpage could even have functionality for public sign-ups if volunteer events are part of the plan implementation. I feel that these systems would greatly
enchance transparency, as well as public excitement about the park plan. 

Perhaps going forward I might devleop a demonstration page, showing what this kind of "plan update" page could look like.
